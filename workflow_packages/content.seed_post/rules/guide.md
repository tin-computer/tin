# The post: a guide that answers one reader question

A seed post turns material the founder already has (meeting notes, a merged pull request, a
draft) into a guide for the people the business serves. The seed supplies the argument. The
post answers a question those readers already ask.

## Reader and argument

- Choose one reader and one question. The question is something that reader would type into a
  search box or ask an AI assistant, in their own words. It is about their problem, not about
  the business or its release.
- The seed is evidence for the answer. A feature, a decision or a lesson in the seed becomes
  the reason the answer is true, the mechanism behind it or the worked example.
- Hold a point of view: a recommendation a reader could question, with its reasons. A competitor
  should not be able to publish the post unchanged.
- State the trade-offs: when the recommendation applies, what it needs, and when another choice
  is better.
- Show the reasoning through something concrete, usually the worked example.
- Respect the reader's expertise. Define terms briefly, only where the reader needs them.
- Lead with what the reader can do afterwards, then explain the function that makes it possible.
  A function label (reporting, dashboards, an integration) describes a tool; the case is the
  work that changes.

## Leading toward the business without a pitch

- The argument shows which part of the answer is hard to keep true in practice: the ongoing,
  cross-team or specialist part. That part is the work the business does.
- The worked example and the checklist follow the way the business works, so a reader who acts
  on them already works that way.
- Name the business at most once in the body, in passing, where the reader's question meets what
  the business does. The closing makes the offer.
- No section about the business and no lone sales line.

## Shape

```text
# <the reader's question, 6 to 14 words>
<the answer: 40 to 70 words, no heading above it>
<the situation the reader recognises: one or two short paragraphs>
## <section heading>            as many sections as the argument needs
## <-ing heading naming the improvement>    the worked example: a table or numbered steps
## <checklist heading>          3 to 5 rows, each "### <title>" and one 15 to 30 word paragraph
## Sources                      only when a cited source supports a point
## <related heading>            links to pages the site has, only when the package lists them
## <closing heading>            30 to 45 words and the site's call to action
```

- The body sections, worked example and checklist together come to the length band the
  recipe names, usually 900 to 1,600 words.
- The answer under the H1 names the subject and repeats the question's terms, so it still reads
  as an answer when quoted alone. Keep its sentences short.
- No byline, FAQ, audience line ("for operations leads"), "In short", "Key takeaways" or summary
  block, and no section numbers.
- Leave out the Sources and related sections when there is nothing real to put in them. Never
  invent a link.

## Headings

- Read in order, the headings tell the post's argument as one story.
- Below the H1, headings are short literal phrases in the reader's words, about 4 to 10 words.
  They are never questions, "How we ..." phrases, slogans or metaphors.
- The worked example's heading names the improvement as an -ing phrase, such as "Taking the
  guesswork out of weekly reporting". It does not contain the word "example".
- No phrase repeats across the headings and the section openings.

## Sections

- Every section opens with its point: the heading and the first sentence state what the section
  claims. Examples, detail and evidence follow.
- Each section adds something new: a reason, a mechanism, an example, evidence or a decision.
- Use a list only for items that are truly parallel or for steps in order. Keep reasoning in prose.

## Two readings before the draft

1. Read the headings alone, in order. They should give a sensible route through the post.
2. Read each heading with its actual opening sentence. Together they should answer the H1 without
   help from paragraphs that are not written yet.

Repair the outline until both readings work.

## Facts and privacy

- Facts come from the package only: the seed and the business files. Never invent a number, a
  customer, a quote, a result or a feature.
- A seed can carry internal detail: names of staff, customers, internal tools, prices under
  negotiation, unreleased plans, file paths and code identifiers. Keep these out of the post
  unless the seed shows they are public. Describe the team as "our team" and never name a
  founder, employee or advisor.
- Write about what a reader can use today. Plans and promises stay out unless the seed states
  them as public.
