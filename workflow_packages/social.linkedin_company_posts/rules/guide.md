# Posts for a company's LinkedIn page

You write a batch of posts for a company's LinkedIn page. The page speaks as the company, as
"we". Most people see one post at a time in their feed, usually without knowing the company.

## Every post

- **Say what the company is.** Read on its own, each post says what the company does and for
  whom, using the company's own published wording. The feature, scene or story the post is
  about comes in as one example of that. Work it into each post's own story in different
  words: no two posts share that sentence, or any other.
- **Start with the reader.** Open on the reader's situation: what they were doing and what got
  in the way. Then say what the company does about it.
- **One concrete detail.** Each post carries something the reader can picture, taken from the
  sources: a command, a file, a step, a number, a screen.
- **One idea.** Each post covers one thing, and the batch covers different things.
- **The first line works alone.** The feed shows about two lines before "see more". Open with
  the reader's situation or the fact, plainly. No question asked only to get a reaction.
- **Short.** 60 to 170 words, in paragraphs of one to three sentences with a blank line between
  them.
- **End on what the reader can do next,** in one plain sentence. No sales push.
- **Warm and plain,** the way the company's site and writing guide sound.

## Formats

Company posts read like news from the company. Use the formats in the project's formats file
when it has one. Otherwise pick formats that fit what this company can show, such as:

- what the product does, in one picture;
- how one part of it works, step by step;
- one feature in detail, and the work it saves;
- a point of view the company acts on, backed by what it built;
- a question buyers ask, answered;
- a published result or case study;
- how the team works, or a decision it made.

Use at least four different formats in a batch of six.

## Facts

- Use only facts in the sources. Every number in a post or on its card appears in a source.
- Name a customer only when a source is a case study the company published.
- Describe only what the sources show as available, and leave out what is planned or in
  build. A post's news is never that something is missing.
- When the brand guide names more than one audience, each post speaks to one of them with that
  audience's positioning and never blends them. Across the batch, write for each audience the
  sources support.
- No prices, hashtags, emojis, @mentions, em dashes or en dashes.
- No links in the post. A link goes in the first comment.

## The card

Every post goes out with one image, its card. For each post write:

- **card_line:** the one headline the card carries, at most nine words. It supports the post
  and says something different from the post's first line.
- **card_kind:** what the card shows:
  - `type`: the headline alone over the company's own artwork;
  - `screen`: the product's own screen, with sample data;
  - `terminal`: a coding agent session, for a product that works through one;
  - `artwork`: a drawing or photo the company already publishes.
- **card_brief:** one or two sentences for the designer: what the card shows and why. A screen
  uses sample data and never a real customer's.
- **alt_text:** the card described for someone who can't see it, including its headline.

Mix the kinds across the batch: at most two `type` cards. When the product has screens, show
them; a screen is the most convincing picture of what the company does.

## First comment

One short line with the link the post points to, taken from the sources. Leave it empty when
the post points nowhere.

## Writing

- Keep each verb next to the thing it acts on. Write the way a person says it out loud.
- Never use the "it's not X, it's Y" frame or any relative of it ("X isn't Y, it's Z", "not X
  but Y", "X, not Y"). Say what the thing is.
- No idioms, stock phrases or decorative metaphors.
- No sentence that drops its subject or skips the reason that connects it to the last one.
